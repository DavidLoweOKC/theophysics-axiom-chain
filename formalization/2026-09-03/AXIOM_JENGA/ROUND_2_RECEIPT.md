## What changed

Round 1 established fixed-registry nonredundancy.  Round 2 refuted any global
reading of that result.  A premise can disappear from the visible basis in at
least three importantly different ways:

1. **Derivation:** another accepted premise and bridge genuinely derive it.
2. **Reclassification:** the model replaces it with a stronger or differently
   typed premise.
3. **Hiding/fusion:** its work is silently baked into a downstream rule.

Only the first would reduce the semantic commitments.  The third reduces the
number of visible inputs while preserving or increasing the hidden assumptions.

## What we got right

- The original eight-input basis is nonredundant under the original registry.
- The dependency chain exposes localized failure and recovery paths.
- Personhood can become operationally meaningful instead of ornamental if an
  independently defended Personhood-to-Agency bridge is added.

## What we got wrong or overstated

- "Unique minimum" without the qualifier "inside this rule registry and
  candidate universe" is false.
- A smaller visible basis is not automatically a more assumption-efficient
  theory.  Stronger bridges can hide more than they remove.
- Computational recovery does not validate the replacement bridge.

## Defensible version

> The original Divine Order basis is minimal under its declared six-rule
> registry.  Alternative registries can recover the same target closure from
> smaller visible bases, so global uniqueness is not established.  One
> alternative, Personhood-to-Agency, gives the previously ornamental Personhood
> node downstream work and deserves semantic investigation.  Several other
> alternatives merely demonstrate how dependency can be hidden inside stronger
> rules and therefore do not reduce the theory's true assumption load.

replacement needs a ledger containing:

1. Love and Truth as independent primitives;

Closure equivalence alone will not choose among them.  The countermodels and
semantic specification must do that work.
